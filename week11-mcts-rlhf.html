Today's two questions

Part A · How can an agent choose a good move?

It imagines possible futures, sees how they end, and uses these simulated outcomes to decide what to do now.

Part B · How can a model learn what people prefer?

Humans show which of two answers they like. A reward model learns to score similar answers more highly.

These parts both involve learning from feedback, but not in the same way. MCTS uses the outcomes of imagined actions to help it make decisions. Mini-RLHF uses examples of human preferences to train a scoring model. Keep that distinction in mind throughout the lab.

Part A · Concept 1

Monte Carlo Tree Search: what is actually happening?

Monte Carlo means using samples or simulated outcomes rather than calculating every possibility exactly. Tree means a branching structure where each node represents a situation and each branch represents an action. Search means evaluating alternative actions before committing to one.

Current board (root)
↓ Choose an action
Move 1
possible future
Move 2
possible future
Move 3
possible future
↓ Each move leads to more board states, wins, losses or draws

Before putting X on the board, imagine testing each possible move by playing out several possible futures. Some moves lead to wins more often than others. MCTS stores the results of those imaginary games so future choices use accumulated evidence. This is planning through simulation, not predicting the future with certainty.

What does MCTS learn?

It accumulates statistics about explored states or actions, such as visit counts and how favourable their simulated outcomes were. This is not automatically the same as training neural-network weights. In this notebook, the mcts_simple tree is searched through self-play and can be saved and loaded.

Important distinction: Does 10,000 iterations mean it has played 10,000 perfect games? No. It means the search routine is given that iteration budget. What constitutes one iteration is determined by the MCTS implementation. More iterations generally improve estimates, but they do not guarantee optimal play.
Part A · Concept 2

The four stages: follow one imagined play

Stage 1 of 4

Why do we repeat all four?

One simulated game gives very weak evidence: a promising move might lose by chance. Repeating the process lets the search revisit promising actions while occasionally exploring alternatives. Selection decides where to investigate; expansion adds a possibility; simulation supplies a possible outcome; and backpropagation carries that information up through the visited path.

Important vocabulary: Backpropagation here means updating MCTS node statistics along the searched path. It is not neural-network gradient backpropagation.
Part A · Concept 3

Why not always choose the move that has won the most?

Because a move visited only twice can look amazing by luck. Another move tried many times may be more reliably good. A common tree policy, UCT, adds an exploration bonus to estimated value.

UCT ≈ average reward + c √(ln(parent visits) / child visits)
Average reward

“How good has this move looked when we tried it?” This favours exploitation of a promising action.

Exploration bonus

“Have we tested this move enough?” Rarely visited moves can receive a larger bonus, encouraging exploration.

Suppose move A won 8 of 10 simulations and move B won 1 of 1. B has a 100% observed success rate, but we barely know anything about it. UCT balances two ideas: choose what looks good, and keep checking what we are uncertain about. The exploration term gets smaller as that move is visited more often.

Notebook precision: The supplied Tic-Tac-Toe solution instantiates MCTS(...) and includes UCT(...) as a commented alternative. The equation above illustrates common UCT reasoning; it is not a claim that these library classes use identical internal selection rules.
Part A · Interactive

Play against a simulation-based agent

You are X; the browser agent is O. Adjust how many fresh simulated continuations per candidate move the interactive demo evaluates. This is a simplified Monte Carlo rollout demonstration, not the notebook's saved MCTS tree.

What is a rollout?

A rollout is one imaginary continuation of a game from a possible next move until the game finishes. It does not change the real board. The agent temporarily tries a legal O move, then fills remaining squares with simulated X and O moves until somebody wins or the board is full.

  1. Try a candidate: suppose O could move to cell 0.
  2. Simulate the rest: play random legal moves on a copy of the board until X wins, O wins or it is a draw.
  3. Assign an outcome from O’s view: O win = +1, draw = 0, X win = −1.
  4. Repeat: 20 rollouts produce 20 outcomes for that same candidate cell.
  5. Average: add the 20 outcome numbers and divide by 20. Compare the average across candidate moves.

Why multiple rollouts? One possible future is not enough to judge a move. More sampled futures generally improve the estimate, but the strategy used during a rollout also matters.

Where does “Cell 0: −0.40 estimated outcome” come from?

−0.40 is calculated from simulated results, not chosen manually. For example, imagine 10 rollouts for placing O in cell 0 give 2 O wins, 2 draws, 6 X wins.

(2 × +1 + 2 × 0 + 6 × −1) ÷ 10 = (2 − 6) ÷ 10 = −0.40

That means O lost more of its simulated games than it won from this candidate move. It is not a 40% probability of losing. It is an average outcome on a scale from −1 (all losses) to +1 (all wins). Because rollouts are random, refreshing or playing again can change the score. The live breakdown below shows the exact win/draw/loss counts behind each displayed number.

Your turn: select an empty cell.
What is the agent calculating?

It samples possible game endings after each legal O move, then prefers the move with a better estimated outcome.

Try playing with just 1–5 rollouts. One unusual imaginary game may strongly affect the estimated score. Increase the number to 100 or more and compare the results. Averaging more samples typically makes estimates less noisy, but it does not guarantee a win. The continuations here use random legal moves, unlike the full MCTS search in the notebook.

How does a win give useful feedback?

A rollout reaches a terminal board. From O's perspective we can score it as win = +1, draw = 0, loss = −1. Average the sampled results for an action. A move with a higher estimated mean has looked more favourable in these experiments. This simple illustrative score is separate from the implementation details of the library's winner() function.

Part A · Actual notebook

Understanding the notebook code

game = TicTacToe()  # Make an empty board and game rules.
tree = MCTS(game, allow_transpositions=True, training=True)
tree.self_play(iterations=10000)

tree.training = False  # Switch from search/training mode.
tree.self_play()         # Observe self-play after training.
tree.save("tictactoe.mcts")  # Store the search structure.

# Later: load the saved tree for human vs agent play.
tree = MCTS(game, allow_transpositions=True, training=False)
tree.load("tictactoe.mcts")

The game class first defines what counts as a board state, what actions are legal, how to make a move and how to tell whether a game is over. We then pass those rules into MCTS. The search explores many possible games and builds useful statistics. Switching training to false means we now want to use the search rather than keep collecting training experience in the same way. Finally, saving the tree means we can reuse it later.

get_state()

Encodes the board into a representation the search can distinguish. The notebook encodes X, O and blank as numbers.

possible_actions()

Returns the indices of empty squares. The agent cannot legally write over an occupied cell.

take_action(action)

Uses action // 3 for row and action % 3 for column, then swaps the active player.

has_outcome()

Checks three-in-a-row or a filled board. This tells the search when to stop a game.

winner()

Reports the outcome in the game/library's expected format so results can affect statistics.

allow_transpositions=True

Allows the library to account for states that can be reached by multiple action orders; details depend on its implementation.

One code detail to mention: The human-play section uses node.choose_best_action(...) if the current search node has explored children; otherwise the notebook falls back to random.choice(actions). A weak move can therefore reflect an unseen branch, not just “failed learning”.

Part A · CartPole

Time steps are not episodes. Episodes are not search iterations.

CartPole is different from Tic-Tac-Toe: there is no opposing player. The agent selects left (0) or right (1) pushes to keep the pole upright. A single time step is one environment action and transition. An episode is the whole sequence from reset until termination or truncation.

Illustrative animation, not the Gym physics engine.

Episode tracker
0 steps
Pushes made in this illustrative episode

At step 0, the environment has just reset.

In the actual notebook, env.step(action) changes the state and returns termination and truncation flags.

If the cart takes one left action, that is one time step. If it balances for 150 actions and then falls, that whole run is one episode containing 150 steps. If it reaches the environment's 500-step limit, the episode is truncated even without falling. Now, MCTS iterations are yet another thing: how many times the search routine explores simulated possibilities. Never use these terms interchangeably.

Change the episode cap: what actually changes?

The actual CartPole-v1 environment uses a 500-step time limit by default. You must configure a wrapper or environment setting to alter that real limit; changing the slider here only demonstrates the concept.

game = CartPole()
tree = OpenLoopMCTS(game, training=True)
tree.self_play(iterations=1000)  # notebook says: try 10000

# Each real environment transition runs this:
self.current_state, _, self.terminated, self.truncated, _ = self.env.step(action)

# A run ends on either event:
return self.terminated or self.truncated

Why OpenLoopMCTS? CartPole has a continuous-valued state, and outcomes can be sensitive to small changes. Open-loop search focuses on sequences of actions rather than requiring identical states to match at every search node. The notebook tries 1,000 search iterations and suggests 10,000. More search can mean more computation and better estimates, but it is not the same as allowing 10,000 real CartPole steps.

Notebook-specific nuance: The CartPole winner() method returns a specially constructed list using remaining steps to influence the library's backpropagated outcome. This is custom to this exercise and is not Gym's standard reward signal.
Part B · Concept 1

Mini-RLHF: teaching a model your taste

RLHF stands for Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback. In the wider RLHF pipeline, human judgments train a reward model and an RL algorithm can later use that reward to update a language model. This Week 11 notebook stops earlier: it trains a preference reward model and demonstrates Best-of-N selection; it does not run an RL policy-optimisation stage.

1 · Human preference

For one question, identify a chosen answer and a rejected answer.

2 · Reward model

Learn to give the chosen response a higher numerical score.

3 · Use the judge

Score and rank candidate answers without changing the base generator.

In this tutorial, we are not teaching Qwen to generate differently yet. We are teaching a separate judge to score answers according to our examples. Our chosen class preference is short, friendly and direct responses. Once the judge has learned those preferences, we can ask it to choose between answers produced by the same unchanged language model.

Why this matters: a high reward score means “preferred according to the reward model's training signal”, not “objectively true, safe or universally best”.
Part B · Concept 2

One preference pair teaches a comparison

Prompt
How do I make toast?
Chosen (preferred)

“Pop bread in the toaster for 2 minutes. Enjoy!”

Same prompt
How do I make toast?
Rejected (less preferred)

“Well, making toast can be approached in several ways depending on your equipment and taste...”

Notice that the question is identical. This is crucial: we are not judging two different tasks. We are showing the model that for this one task, answer A matches our desired style better than answer B. A single pair tells it little; many varied and consistent pairs let it learn a pattern that might transfer to questions it has never seen.

Why add 15–20 of your own pairs?

The notebook asks students to add 15–20 pairs consistent with the class theme. Variety helps distinguish a general preference from one memorised wording. Repeating identical examples increases their influence but does not add new information.

45 student-example presentations

The multiplier does not create new opinions. If we have 15 student pairs and repeat them three times, those preferences appear 45 times in the training list. That makes them more influential relative to the supplied examples. Too much repetition can increase overfitting to our exact phrases, so more is not always better.

Part B · Concept 3

How does the reward model know what to change?

The reward model gives each answer a score, for example chosen = 1.2 and rejected = 0.3. It cares primarily about the difference, not whether the raw score is positive. Training tries to make chosen score greater than rejected score.

loss = −log σ(r(chosen) − r(rejected))

Read the equation from inside out: (1) subtract the scores to find the margin; (2) sigmoid turns that margin into a value between 0 and 1; (3) negative log creates a penalty that becomes small when chosen clearly outranks rejected.

Margin
+1.00
Preference probability
73.1%
Pairwise loss
0.313

Imagine the judge scores our preferred answer 1.0 and the rejected answer 0.0. The gap is positive, which is the correct order, but the model could be more confident. If we increase that gap, the loss gets smaller. If the chosen answer scores lower, the margin becomes negative and the loss increases. Gradient-based optimisation uses that loss to update the trainable parameters so it ranks future pairs better.

Part B · Concept 4

What changes when you increase epochs?

An epoch means one pass through the training dataset. If you have 100 training pairs, four epochs means the trainer processes those examples through four passes (with batching), not that it receives 400 distinct preference judgments.

EarlyMiddleLaterHeld-out

Think of learning a dance sequence. One rehearsal may be insufficient. Repeating helps, but rehearsing the same sequence forever does not guarantee you can perform a completely new routine. Epochs are repeated exposure to the same preference pairs. Initially, more epochs may improve rankings. Eventually they may stop helping, or the model may overfit and perform worse on questions it has never seen. We judge success using held-out examples, not training loss alone.

Batch size, gradient accumulation, learning rate

Batch size = 4

Process four preference examples in a mini-batch before computing its gradient contribution.

Accumulation = 2

Combine gradients across two mini-batches before an optimiser step. Effective batch is about 8 examples per step for one device.

Learning rate = 0.0001

Controls the scale of parameter updates. Too large can destabilise training; too small can slow improvement.

Do not treat the visual chart as a real training result. It illustrates possible behaviour, not an experiment with the uploaded Qwen reward-model checkpoints. Run the Colab notebook to observe actual loss and accuracy.
Part B · Concept 5

How do we know if the model learned a preference?

Compare before vs after training using prompts excluded from training. For each pair, the judge is correct if r(chosen) > r(rejected). Pairwise accuracy is the proportion of held-out pairs ranked correctly. A random-order baseline is around 50%, though a particular untrained model can score above or below chance.

Do not say 'training worked' just because the training loss decreased. Ask it to judge examples it never practised. If it gets those correct more often after training, that is stronger evidence that it learned a general preference rather than memorising the dataset. This notebook also compares several random untrained scoring heads, because one random head can look lucky.

Best-of-N: pick the best answer without retraining the generator

The notebook generates 8 candidate answers with the unchanged base language model, scores each with the trained reward judge, and returns the candidate with the highest score. It is selection, not RL fine-tuning.

With one candidate, there is nothing to compare. With eight candidates, we can choose the one our reward model likes best. That often gives a better match to our preferences, but we need to generate eight outputs every time and the generator itself has not learned anything new. A bad reward model can even select the wrong answer very confidently.

What if I flip chosen and rejected?

Now long, detailed answers become preferred in the training pairs. With enough consistent examples and successful training, the reward model may learn the opposite ranking. This shows reward scores are learned from the specified preference signal, not a universal definition of a good answer.

What if the reward model gives a nonsense answer a high score?

It has found a shortcut or failed to generalise. The reward model is an imperfect proxy for human judgement. Test diverse held-out examples, inspect surprising rankings and improve preference data rather than assuming high score means high quality.

Is RLHF itself being run here?

The notebook is titled “Mini RLHF”, but its implemented practical stages are preference data, reward-model training, evaluation, Best-of-N ranking and saving the model. The base generator is not updated with PPO or another RL optimisation algorithm in this notebook.

Key concepts to remember

Two different approaches to using feedback appear in this lab. In MCTS, an agent considers possible actions, simulates futures and uses outcomes to guide choices. It repeats selection, expansion, simulation and backpropagation. More iterations can improve its estimates, but searching is not the same as taking real environment steps.

In mini-RLHF, a reward model is shown pairs of answers and learns which answer people prefer. Its loss encourages chosen answers to have higher scores than rejected ones. More epochs means more passes through the same preference examples, but we must test on unseen examples to check generalisation. Finally, Best-of-N uses that learned judge to select an answer; it does not retrain the generator. Three distinctions are especially important: searching is not training, time steps are not episodes, and choosing an answer is not the same as improving the generator.
