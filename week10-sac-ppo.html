1. What problem are we solving?

Imagine a simulated robot with two legs. We want it to walk forward without falling. Nobody gives it a list of the correct movements. It has to discover useful movements by trying them and seeing what happens.

What the robot receives

Observation

The walker receives 24 numbers describing things such as its body angle, movement, leg joints and ground measurements. This is the information available for its next decision.

What the robot chooses

Action

It outputs four continuous motor commands, each between −1 and 1, for the hips and knees. Continuous means it can choose intermediate amounts, rather than only “on” or “off”.

The environment gives a reward: moving forward earns reward, motor effort has a small cost, and falling has a large penalty. An illustrative action such as [0.2, −0.4, 0.1, 0.3] means four motor commands, not four reward scores.

Learn which movements lead to a good whole walk, even when the benefit or mistake becomes clear only several moments later.

For example, lifting a leg may give little immediate reward, yet make the next step possible. Leaning forward may look useful immediately, yet cause a fall later. The learner must connect present decisions to future results.

One step is one interaction. An episode is one complete attempt, from reset until it ends. Reinforcement learning, or RL, improves decisions using the rewards from these attempts.

2. The actor, critic and their learning signals

A policy is the rule that chooses an action from an observation. Here, a neural network learns that rule by adjusting its internal numbers, called weights.

Actor: the decision maker

“Given what I can see, which movement should I try?”

A stochastic actor describes a distribution of possible actions. It can sample different movements in the same situation.

Critic: the predictor

“How much future reward do I expect?”

It learns by comparing its predictions with targets built from the experience collected.

TermPlain meaningSmall example
RewardFeedback from one stepThis step earns +1.
ReturnRewards added over time, usually discountedRewards 1, 1, 1 with γ = 0.9 give 1 + 0.9 + 0.81 = 2.71.
Value, V(s)Predicted return from a situation“From this balanced pose, I expect 8.”

Gamma, γ, controls the weight of future rewards. A value near 1 keeps later rewards important. Learning rate controls the size of a weight adjustment. A batch is a small group of experiences used for one learning update. An epoch is one pass through the training data being reused.

The actor chooses; the critic predicts. The learning signal tells the actor which choices to favour next time.

In these notebooks, SAC uses two Q critics. PPO uses a V critic and advantages. They solve the same walking task using different learning signals and different rules for reusing data.

3. SAC: learn from memory and keep exploring

Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) trains an actor and two action-value critics. “Soft” refers to the entropy term: the learner values a useful range of possible actions alongside reward.

Start with a memory of attempts

After each interaction, SAC stores (observation, action, reward, next observation, episode boundary) in a replay buffer. It samples small batches from this memory to learn.

Think of reviewing earlier practice attempts. An attempt can still teach you something after your technique changes. This reuse of data collected by older policies is called off-policy learning.

Why two critics?

Predictions can be too optimistic. Suppose one critic predicts 12 and the other predicts 8 for a movement. SAC uses the smaller estimate, 8, in its learning targets and actor objective. This helps reduce overestimation; it does not make every prediction correct.

Entropy: how spread out are the possible choices?

For a simple two-choice example, compare an actor that chooses left almost every time with an actor that gives left and right equal chances.

Lower entropy
90% left10%

One choice dominates. There is little variety.

Higher entropy
50% left50% right

Choices are more spread out. There is more variety.

This picture uses two discrete choices to explain the idea. The walker actually uses a distribution over continuous motor commands.

Why include entropy? Early in training, one movement may look best simply because the robot has tried very few alternatives. Encouraging variety helps it avoid settling too early on a poor walking pattern.

Actor goal ≈ high predicted return + α × entropy

Alpha, α, balances the two terms. A larger alpha gives variety more weight. In Part A, ent_coef="auto" learns alpha to track a target entropy. This is an exploration setting, not a bonus physically paid by the environment.

Optional: what the entropy formula means
H = −Σ p(a) log p(a)

For discrete actions, compute this across possible actions. A 50/50 distribution has more entropy than 90/10. For the walker’s continuous actions, SAC uses log probability density instead; the numbers are not ordinary probabilities and entropy need not be positive.

How one learning update works

  1. Sample stored transitions. Each transition says what happened after an action.
  2. Train both critics. Their target includes the observed reward and an estimate of what can happen next.
  3. Train the actor. Favour actions with high predicted Q while retaining entropy.
  4. Update the supporting estimates. Slowly move target critics towards the trained critics, and adjust alpha when automatic tuning is enabled.

Why do we need target critics?

A critic predicts future return. To improve that prediction, we need a number to compare it with: a training target. Part of that target is the reward we observed; the rest comes from a prediction of what can happen next.

This creates a difficulty: we are using predictions to train predictions. If the same rapidly changing network supplies both sides, changing its weights can change the answer it is trying to learn. An overly optimistic prediction can feed into the next target and reinforce itself.

A small example of the moving target

Consider reward 1 and γ = 0.9. Leave out the entropy term here to focus on the future-value prediction.

What happensNext-state Q estimateTarget for this transition
Before an update101 + 0.9 × 10 = 10
The trained critic’s next-state estimate jumps20Using that changing critic directly gives 1 + 0.9 × 20 = 19.
Use a slower target copy insteadThe target copy changes more gradually.The target is less exposed to that sudden weight change.

The observed transition has not changed, yet the target could jump from 10 to 19. A target critic is a separate copy that follows the trained critic slowly. It supplies the future Q estimate, giving the trained critic a steadier reference to learn towards.

How does the target copy move?

target weight ← (1 − τ) × old target weight + τ × trained critic weight

Part A uses tau=0.02: keep 98% of the old target weight and mix in 2% of the trained weight. For one illustrative weight, if the target has 8 and the trained critic has 12:

new target weight = 0.98 × 8 + 0.02 × 12 = 8.08

The copy moves towards 12 rather than jumping straight to it. These numbers illustrate a network weight; the full network’s Q output is not necessarily the same weighted average.

Two trained critics

Learn Q values from batches. Using the smaller Q estimate helps reduce overestimation.

Two target copies

Follow those critics slowly. They supply next-state Q estimates when building critic targets.

The actor is trained using the current trained critics. Target critics support critic learning; they do not choose the robot’s movement. They make the Q part of the target steadier, while the actor and entropy coefficient can still change.

Optional: connect that explanation to the SAC target
target = reward + γ × [min(target_Q1, target_Q2) − α × log π(next_action | next_state)]

The next action is sampled from the current actor. Both target Q values evaluate it. At a true terminal state there is no future term. Time-limit truncations need appropriate bootstrapping and are handled by the library.

4. PPO: change the policy carefully

Proximal Policy Optimisation (PPO) collects a recent batch of experience, called a rollout. It then learns from that batch several times before collecting fresh experience.

Why can a large policy change be a problem?

Imagine the robot tries a leg movement during a few successful walks. That movement looks useful, so we want it to become more likely. But a small batch cannot tell us that it should become almost the only movement the robot ever tries.

If we make it much more likely after just those attempts, the robot may lose other useful movements and start falling. PPO therefore limits how much extra improvement a sample can claim from a large change. This matters because PPO makes several learning passes through the same recent data.

First understand the ratio

Choose one observation and one action that was actually sampled. Compare how likely that same action is under the new policy and the policy that collected the rollout.

ratio = new likelihood ÷ old likelihood
Old probabilityNew probabilityRatioMeaning
20%20%1.00Unchanged
20%22%1.1010% more likely relative to the old policy
20%30%1.5050% more likely relative to the old policy

These are discrete probabilities to make the arithmetic visible. The walker has continuous actions, so its policy uses probability densities. The same ratio idea applies.

What does “clip” mean?

To clip a number is to replace values beyond an interval with the nearest endpoint. Part B sets clip_range=0.18, giving endpoints 1 − 0.18 = 0.82 and 1 + 0.18 = 1.18.

Actual ratioClipped copyWhat the clipping calculation does
0.600.82Below the lower endpoint: use 0.82 in the clipped branch.
1.101.10Inside the interval: keep it.
1.501.18Above the upper endpoint: use 1.18 in the clipped branch.

The 18% is a relative change in likelihood. Starting from 20%, the upper endpoint corresponds to 20% × 1.18 = 23.6%, rather than adding 18 percentage points to make 38%.

Where is the clipped number used?

PPO uses it to calculate a learning score. The optimiser changes the actor’s weights to improve this score. A score is a mathematical training signal, not the reward returned by the environment.

The advantage judges the sampled action: positive means better than expected, negative means worse than expected. PPO calculates two versions of the actor score:

  1. Ordinary: actual ratio × advantage.
  2. Clipped: clipped copy of the ratio × advantage.
  3. Choose the smaller score. This prevents the objective from offering extra improvement for a helpful-looking change that has gone too far.

Follow one good action all the way through

The new probability can still be 30%. PPO has capped this sample’s learning score at 2.36. It has not reset the policy probability to 23.6%, and it has not clipped a motor command.

What if the action was worse than expected?

For a bad action, we want its likelihood to decrease. Use advantage −2, old probability 20% and new probability 12%. The ratio is 12 ÷ 20 = 0.60.

Ordinary score = 0.60 × (−2) = −1.20
Clipped score = 0.82 × (−2) = −1.64
PPO chooses min(−1.20, −1.64) = −1.64

Higher scores are better, including when scores are negative. Without clipping, reducing the bad action’s probability further would keep making the score less negative. PPO caps that improvement once the ratio falls below 0.82.

Why keep both scores and take the minimum? If a bad action becomes more likely instead, its ordinary score becomes worse. PPO keeps that penalty. It caps overly large changes in the direction that looks helpful, while preserving the penalty for moving in the wrong direction.

AdvantageHelpful changeWhere improvement flattens
PositiveMake the action more likelyRatio above 1.18
NegativeMake the action less likelyRatio below 0.82

Other samples and shared network weights can still move the policy beyond these endpoints. Clipping removes an incentive for large changes in this objective; it does not impose a hard probability boundary or guarantee better performance.

The four lines that explain clipping

Core actor-objective logic used inside PPO, shown for explanation. advantages are normally normalised in these SB3 runs. This is not code to paste beside model.learn().

ratio = torch.exp(log_prob - old_log_prob)
ordinary = ratio * advantages
clipped = torch.clamp(ratio, 1 - clip_range, 1 + clip_range) * advantages
policy_loss = -torch.min(ordinary, clipped).mean()

log_prob stores the new log likelihood; old_log_prob stores the fixed old one. Exponentiating their difference gives the ratio. The minus sign turns a score to maximise into a loss to minimise. This is the actor term; PPO also trains the value critic and can include an entropy term.

5. GAE: decide how good each action was

PPO needs an advantage for each action: was this choice better or worse than we expected? We cannot judge every action from its immediate reward alone because its effects may appear later.

Generalised Advantage Estimation (GAE) builds that judgement by combining prediction errors from nearby steps. The calculation has two steps.

Step 1: calculate the one-step surprise

surprise δ = reward + γ × predicted next value − predicted current value

Suppose the critic predicted 8 before the movement. The movement earns 1, and the critic predicts 10 from the next position. With γ = 0.9:

δ = 1 + 0.9 × 10 − 8 = +2

The result looks 2 better than the critic expected. This temporal-difference error is a useful signal, but it depends on an imperfect next-value prediction.

Step 2: combine several surprises

Use surprises +2, +1, −1 from three successive steps. To make the arithmetic easy, give each later step half the weight of the previous one:

advantage estimate = 2 + 0.5 × 1 + 0.25 × (−1) = 2.25

The positive middle surprise helps the first action; the later negative surprise reduces that judgement. This connects a movement with consequences that appear after it.

Visualise the weighted contributions

This three-step example ends after the last surprise. Its default q = 0.5 is chosen for easy arithmetic, not taken from the notebook.

What does lambda do?

Lambda, λ, controls how much GAE includes later errors. With λ = 0, it uses only the one-step error. A value closer to 1 includes more of the later evidence.

Using more future evidence reduces reliance on the critic’s immediate prediction, but also brings in more variation from what happened later. Lambda balances those two effects. Gamma still controls discounting; the weight between successive errors is their product, γλ.

Part B uses γ = 0.999 and λ = 0.95, so the actual multiplier is 0.94905. GAE is calculated separately across episode boundaries so one attempt is not mixed with the next.

Equation and the backward calculation

continues[t] is 0 at an episode boundary and 1 otherwise. next_values[t] is 0 at true termination; where the task can continue, it uses the critic’s next-state estimate.

Aₜ = δₜ + (γλ)δₜ₊₁ + (γλ)²δₜ₊₂ + …
Equivalent backward calculation: Aₜ = δₜ + γλ × Aₜ₊₁
# Work backwards through the rollout
advantage = 0
for t in reversed(range(len(rewards))):
    delta = rewards[t] + gamma * next_values[t] - values[t]
    advantage = delta + gamma * gae_lambda * continues[t] * advantage
    advantages[t] = advantage
returns = advantages + values

Who uses the result? The actor uses the advantage to favour or discourage a sampled action. The critic learns to predict a return target, built here as advantage + old value. A positive advantage does not mean an action is always good, only that it looks better than the current baseline in this data.

With one environment, the rollout contains 2,048 steps. Batches of 64 make 32 batches per epoch. Ten epochs mean 320 minibatch updates per rollout. The old likelihoods stay fixed throughout those passes. ent_coef=0.0 removes PPO’s explicit entropy-loss term; the policy can still sample actions.

6. PPO with pictures: what the custom CNN adds

Part C still uses PPO. What changes is the observation: the agent sees Pong images rather than a vector of body measurements. A convolutional neural network (CNN) turns those images into useful numerical features.

A convolution applies a small learned filter across an image. Different filters can learn useful patterns such as edges, paddle locations or ball-related features. The network learns which patterns matter from the training signal; we do not manually label each object.

Why stack four frames?

One image shows where the ball is. Several recent images help reveal where it is moving. If its horizontal position is 20, 24, 28, 32 across four frames, it is moving right. If the order is 32, 28, 24, 20, it is moving left.

Keep the important CNN code

Core excerpt from CustomCNN; the surrounding class and n_flatten calculation are in the notebook.

self.cnn = nn.Sequential(
    nn.Conv2d(observation_space.shape[0], 32, kernel_size=8, stride=4),
    nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Conv2d(32, 64, kernel_size=4, stride=2), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Conv2d(64, 64, kernel_size=3, stride=1), nn.ReLU(),
    nn.Flatten(),
)
self.linear = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(n_flatten, 512), nn.ReLU())

def forward(self, observations):
    return self.linear(self.cnn(observations))

kernel_size is the filter’s width and height. stride is how far it moves each time. ReLU introduces a nonlinear transformation so the network can learn more than a single linear mapping. Flatten turns the feature maps into a vector. The linear layer converts that vector to 512 features.

Connect the CNN to PPO

Condensed Part C setup. The game name is the one used in the supplied notebook; available environment IDs depend on the installed Atari package.

env = make_atari_env("PongNoFrameskip-v4", n_envs=8, seed=0)
env = VecFrameStack(env, n_stack=4)
model = PPO(
    "CnnPolicy", env,
    policy_kwargs={"features_extractor_class": CustomCNN},
    n_steps=128, batch_size=256, n_epochs=4,
    learning_rate=2.5e-4, clip_range=0.1,
    ent_coef=0.01, vf_coef=0.5,
)

Eight environments each collect 128 steps, giving 1,024 transitions per rollout. With batch size 256 and four epochs, that is 16 minibatch updates. Eight environment instances do not necessarily mean eight separate processes.

ent_coef=0.01 adds an exploration term to PPO’s loss. vf_coef=0.5 weights the critic’s prediction loss. Here, the clip interval is 0.9 to 1.1. The CNN changes how observations are represented, while PPO controls how the policy learns.

7. Read results and compare fairly

model.learn() interacts with the environment and updates weights. model.predict() chooses an action from an observation. model.save() stores the model for later use. Installation, video display and callback boilerplate support the workflow, but do not explain how the algorithms learn.

Result or logHow to explain it
Episode reward / ep_rew_meanTotal reward over complete attempts, usually averaged. Higher is desirable, but check several evaluation episodes.
ep_len_meanAverage attempt length. A longer episode might mean surviving longer, but does not automatically mean better walking.
clip_fractionFraction of sampled ratios outside the clip interval. It does not mean motor commands were clipped or every one of these samples was on the objective’s flat branch.
